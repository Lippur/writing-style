# Strict mode

Read this file if the user asks for strict or full STE. Also read it for a procedure, a safety instruction or maintenance documentation. The rules paraphrase ASD-STE100. They are not the official text. Compliance work needs the official specification and dictionary (https://www.asd-ste100.org). Call the result "in STE style", never "STE-compliant".

## 1. Words

- Use only three kinds of words: approved dictionary words, technical nouns and technical verbs.
- Use an approved word only as its approved part of speech and with its approved meaning. "Test" and "check" are nouns: write "Do a test of the pump", not "Test the pump". "About" means "concerned with". Use "approximately" for numbers.
- Technical nouns name things in your subject: parts, tools, materials, systems, software, units, documents, colours and damage types. Use the term that your field or company approves, and one term for one item. Do not use a technical noun as a verb: write "apply oil to the valve", not "oil the valve".
- Technical verbs name specific processes: manufacturing (drill, solder) and computer actions (click, upload, delete). Do not use them as nouns.
- Choose short, easy technical nouns. Do not use slang or regional words.
- Use British spelling.

## 2. Multi-word nouns

- A multi-word noun has three words or fewer.
- If a technical name has more than three words, write it in full once. Then use a short form, or hyphenate the words that act as one unit.

## 3. Verbs

- Permitted forms: infinitive, imperative, simple present, simple past, simple future ("will"), and the past participle as an adjective only ("the installed module", or "the module is installed" as a state).
- Do not use perfect tenses ("has opened", "had been"), progressive tenses ("is running") or modal-plus-passive forms ("can be adjusted", "is to be installed").
- Use "-ing" forms only as part of a technical noun ("logging level") or in titles.
- Use the active voice in procedures. In descriptions, use the passive only if you do not know the actor.
- Put the action in an approved verb: write "install the module", not "do the installation of the module". If the action word is approved only as a noun ("test", "check"), write "do a test of" or "do a check of".

## 4. Sentences

- Do not leave out words to shorten a sentence. Keep articles, "that" and verbs. Do not use contractions.
- Use a vertical list for complex information.
- Use connecting words to link sentences on related topics.
- Put "the", "a", "an", "this" or "these" before a noun where English needs it.

## 5. Procedures

- A procedural sentence has 20 words or fewer. This includes warnings and cautions.
- Give one instruction in each sentence. Two actions can share a sentence only if they occur at the same time.
- Write instructions in the imperative ("Remove the cover.").
- If the reader must know a condition first, start with the condition, then a comma, then the command ("If the light comes on, stop the engine.").
- Notes give information only, never instructions. A sentence in a note has 25 words or fewer.

## 6. Descriptions

- Do not use the imperative in descriptive text.
- Give information gradually, with one subject in each sentence.
- Use key words to show the logical structure ("first", "then", "as a result").
- A descriptive sentence has 25 words or fewer.
- Each paragraph has one topic and six sentences at most.

## 7. Safety instructions

- A **WARNING** is for a risk of injury or death. A **CAUTION** is for a risk of damage to equipment, data or property.
- Use the signal word for the level of risk.
- Start with a short command or condition. Then give the risk or the result.

> **WARNING:** Disconnect the power supply before you remove the cover. The terminals have a voltage that can kill you.

## 8. Punctuation and word count

- All standard punctuation is permitted, except the semicolon.
- Use hyphens to join words that are directly related ("high-pressure pump").
- Parentheses are permitted for references, item numbers, step numbers, abbreviations, singular-and-plural "(s)", short explanations and alternatives.
- Word count:
  - A colon at the end of a list introduction ends the sentence.
  - Text in parentheses counts as one word.
  - A number, a number with its unit, an abbreviation, an alphanumeric identifier, quoted text, a title or label, and a proper noun each count as one word.
  - A hyphenated word counts as one word.

## 9. Writing practices

- If a word-for-word swap does not work, rebuild the sentence.
- Do not use phrasal verbs ("put on" becomes "install" or "attach").
- Keep terms and wording consistent across the whole document.
- Keep "that" after verbs such as "make sure" and "show".
- Use "with" carefully, because it can mean "by", "has" or "near".
- Make each pronoun point to one clear noun. Do not use "this" alone: write "this value" or "this step".
- Do not use Latin abbreviations. Write "for example", "that is" and "and other" for "e.g.", "i.e." and "etc.".
- Do not use possessives for things: write "the cover of the pump", not "the pump's cover".
- Use inclusive language.

## 10. Final checklist

- [ ] No procedural sentence has more than 20 words. No descriptive sentence has more than 25.
- [ ] No paragraph has more than six sentences or more than one topic.
- [ ] Every instruction is imperative and active, and has one action.
- [ ] No perfect, progressive or modal-passive verb forms.
- [ ] No "-ing" verb forms outside technical nouns.
- [ ] No semicolons, contractions or Latin abbreviations.
- [ ] No noun stack has more than three words.
- [ ] "Check" and "test" occur as nouns only.
- [ ] The only modal verbs are "can", "must" and "will".
- [ ] Each thing has one name across the whole text.
- [ ] Each warning and caution comes before the step that it protects.
- [ ] Every fact, number and identifier from the source is still there, at the same strength.
